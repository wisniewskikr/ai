# S03E01 · Mechanics Behind Product Engineering

Even if language model development stopped today, it's reasonable to say there is no going back to manual coding and that we can build pretty much anything we want. But **not everything**, especially given what we've learned about building production systems that need to be reliable and efficient. It's just that **our role isn't about writing code anymore. And if it's not, what is?**

If we look at the software around us, we'll see that AI can replicate most of its features quite easily, even through reverse engineering. But getting the entire product right is something that can't be vibe coded with a couple of prompts, or even a fleet of agents. That's because it isn't just about writing code, the architecture, the business perspective, or making individual decisions. **It's about doing all of this well, at the right time.**

And that's where product engineering begins.

## Outline

- Shift: outcome over output, product engineer vs. product manager, attention as the bottleneck
- Ownership: scope, user impact, business value, technical consequences
- Judgment: assumptions, tradeoffs, taste, edge cases, uncertainty, consequences
- Systems Thinking: architecture, dependencies, operational impact, system diffs
- Leverage: AI-assisted exploration, looped prototyping, generative exploration, synthetic inputs

## The Shift

Let's assume advances in generative AI continue to the point where **the entire development process** becomes autonomous. Agents can take on any requirement or issue and handle it well. They take care of quality, edge cases and performance, make good architecture decisions and even add the kind of polish many believe requires taste. To put it plainly: **almost everything about our job gets automated**.

What then?

Well, nobody knows this for sure, but if we think about it, when everyone can get almost anything they ask for, the difference comes from **deciding what to build**, **how good the result will be**, **and how fast it can evolve**. Beside that, there are things that cannot be generated by LLM, such as **real world data, user base, clients** or **reputation**. 

Everything combined gives us a good product that can't be easily cloned. Because even if someone builds the same feature in a single day, they won't get our clients the same day. And it works the other way around too: if someone has the users, it doesn't mean they need the features we have.

For anyone already familiar with building products from more than just an engineering standpoint, all of this may sound like nothing new. There is a difference, though: **velocity** and **adaptability**. Both need to reach levels unlike anything we've seen before, not just in generating code but in every single aspect of product development. Today, adding a new feature to a product can be fast, but adding **the right feature done well** is an entirely different game.

There are a few reasons behind this, and one of them is that **agents are becoming a proxy between users and apps**. One example is **Google Search**, which many of us now use through agents rather than directly. Another example is ffmpeg, a tool that agents often use without even telling us. The same goes for [Sentry](https://sentry.io/welcome/), [Resend](https://resend.com/), [Parallel](https://parallel.ai/), [Kitesurf](https://blog.cloudflare.com/kitesurf/), [e2b](https://e2b.dev/), [AgentMail](https://www.agentmail.to/), [1Password](https://1password.com/) and many, many others. And all these are services and tools that can't be easily generated.

All this suggests that software **will be needed** even in an agentic world, and the need for it will probably grow, because a single person **won't have just one agent, but entire fleets of them**. This raises a new set of challenges related not only to software, but also to hardware and most of the processes involved in building products. And those challenges are **extremely exciting** from an engineering perspective.

With this in mind, let's return to where we are today. What we've seen so far suggests that autonomy is already achievable in many areas, but it remains very demanding and runs into real technical barriers. Addressing them requires an understanding not only of the technology, but also of the broader context of the product. So the question is no longer "**is the ticket done?**" but "**can the user now do what they need?**". 

That is the shift from **output to outcome.**

## Ownership

Responsibility is one of the core aspects of our work because we care about quality, maintainability, scalability, performance, security and the countless details that go into delivering great software. Today, agents can handle most of these, but not entirely on their own, because we still need to make many critical decisions about the shape of the system. 

In **S02E02** we've talked about agentic primitives, a generalized building blocks, as a critical aspect of the architecture that unlocks way to autonomous development and self-evolving software. Primitives, in practice, aren't just an engineering challenge, as a deep understanding of domain, product and the users is equally important. 

Great example of this comes from [Jediah Katz talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7Y4sbZgBdnc&t=969s) about harnesses and Grok Bot. He brings **Subscriptions** as a **primitive** that lets the agents handle **email, communicators, pr reviews, alerts and schedules**. This single building block is enough for handling all of them, and anything else that can be subscribed and send wake signals of any form.

On the right, a white drop-shaped agent mascot with an alarm clock, a bell, an envelope and a chat bubble. [Open figure](https://hq.mega.dev/figures/s03e01/artifacts/lesson/figure-3/frame.html)

The decision about the **Subscriptions** primitive seems obvious in this case. But if we look a little bit closer at features such as **permissions, routing, dedupe, filtering and waking**, and at the fact that this single primitive handles not just messaging apps but many kinds of sources, it becomes clear that whoever came up with it **knew exactly what the broader picture of the entire product looked like**. Judging by an architectural decision like this, they also seem to have had decent engineering experience.

While generalization in software engineering isn't anything new, it's easy to imagine that this feature started as a request to **integrate our app with e-mail and calendar**. Without broader context, we as engineers might anticipate that there would be more than one integration and build some sort of gateway and adapters. Even though this would work, it would be a trap compared with the **Subscriptions** primitive, because it would lock us out of other types of composition.

Thinking in primitives clearly shows us the difference between **output and outcome**. The first led us to an implemented feature but added weight to the architecture, which quickly slowed us down. The second **extended the product's capabilities** while keeping the architecture lean and flexible. It also made adding new subscriptions so easy that agents can manage them at runtime themselves. In fact, this is one of Grok Bot's features.

All this **does not come on its own** and it requires from us **additional effort** as much simpler it is to just implement the feature we were asked for. At the same time, finding a proper primitive require from us **taking ownership** and seeking for additional context that may come from business, users and our own research. 

This may sound as if we're becoming product managers, but the difference between that role and a product engineer is that a **PM decides which problem to solve and why**, while a **PE decides what the product must do to solve it.**

The diagram above shows that product engineering isn't just about good primitives, but also about everything required to make them and the results we get once they're in place. Besides, this way of thinking applies not only to architecture decisions, but to nearly every single aspect of a product, with questions such as **"should this feature exist?"**, **"is this still needed?"**, **"how can these features be combined?"**, **"what brings the most value to users?"** or **"what makes users churn?"**

And to be clear, **we're not talking about changing our roles** or rebuilding products from scratch, but rather about shifting the way we think, work, make decisions and build. We're talking more about a **smooth transition**, not necessarily an instant switch. Especially because **most codebases and most products aren't ready yet for autonomous development**. 

During the transition, we may observe that:

- The **product** becomes better aligned with users' actual needs and stays more competitive because it can adapt faster, thanks to better primitives and the system we build around it. That system takes care of quality not only in engineering processes, but also in other areas such as marketing and customer success.
- **Users** start to notice that the product evolves faster than before and becomes more customizable and accessible, especially if we find a way to let their agents interact with it.
- The **codebase** gradually becomes less complex thanks to proper generalization and the removal of features that can be either replaced with simpler ones or easily retired. Moreover, agents will take over nearly every single process, because the environment we shape for them lets them reliably verify the results of their work and give us actual proof of it.
- The **business** gets more tools to reach out to new clients and increase the lifetime value of existing users, which translates almost directly into healthier cash flow and new opportunities to maintain sustainable growth.

There will be many more signals revealing the effects of our work, and those signals will bring us new ideas and new challenges to solve. 

## Judgement

As engineers, we do know how to create good software. In many ways, AI has made this much easier, but at the same time, it has added many new components and changed many of the rules. Today we can process data in ways that weren't feasible 3 years ago, while features that were once valuable enough to build entire products around can now be generated by anyone.

Even though AI can write better code than most human programmers, our knowledge, experience and many of our skills are still useful, especially if we understand how engineering is changing because of the new possibilities we all have now. 

Given the definition of a Product Engineer, we need to find a way to work much more broadly. That means reaching into other areas of the product and the business, or digging into low-level details or even technologies we are not familiar with. And while this may sound extremely difficult, most of the knowledge we need is one prompt away. We just need to learn how to explore **unknown unknowns**.

Exploring an entirely new topic isn't easy when we don't know what we want to learn, or even where to look. But LLMs can do more than answer our questions. They may also suggest [questions we didn't know we wanted to ask](https://github.com/overment/skills/tree/main/skills/unknown-unknowns). On top of that, we can create a skill that tightens the feedback loop, so instead of throwing a wall of text at us, the AI guides us with short, well-phrased messages. And whenever we need it, the agent can give us a mind map or interactive artifacts presenting **wireframes** or **proofs of concept**.

The same way we can discuss and quickly iterate on database structures, primitives, or anything else that's useful to see from a broad perspective, with the option to zoom in on the details.

Agents can also help us understand and make decisions about the product and features we're about to build. In this case, however, we can't rely on the model's base knowledge alone, and even the codebase often isn't enough, since it lacks the context needed to understand the decisions we have to make. That's why we discussed knowledge systems in **S01E03**: once we have them, we can spin up a couple of agents with different models to work together in T3 Code/Devin/Cursor or Herdr on an .html document that presents the choices we have.

In such documents, we can ask the agents to use well-known decision-making tools such as a **feasibility matrix**, an Eisenhower matrix, a decision tree, or a pre-mortem analysis. Below is an example that presents features in terms of their value, complexity and risk, so either we or an agent can decide which of them to pick based on the available resources and the broader context.

Undoubtedly, we have many tools, and we can create new ones that are perfectly tailored to our needs. But good judgment isn't about getting things right the first time. It's about deciding what to do next so we uncover as many unknowns as possible, while using the resources we have efficiently and learning from what we've done so far.

AI let us build things fast, but building for a production is still hard and takes time to get it right. As we'll experience sooner or later, having a fleet of agents working for us almost around the clock, brings us new challenges including those not strictly related with agents. 

## Leverage

Primitives, ownership, better architecture, proper context management, parallel agents and the entire product engineering can get us to a place where development happens insanely fast. After **S01E05** we also know that at the core of everything else is taking care of the **environment that let's agents verify their own work**.

But that's all can be useless...if we build the wrong thing.

There's a great quote from [Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal) book, saying that "*the best designers and the best programmers aren't the ones with the best skills or the nimblest fingers (...), they are the ones that can determine **what just doesn't matter.** That's where the real gains are made*". 

That book was written in 2006, and this part remains relevant and becomes even more important, as today we can move fast, at the wrong direction. And while it's easy now to simply remove code, we can't easily rollback things that were already shipped to the users, as here we're dealing with **their trust.** 

A leverage we're looking for, especially when we want to move from direct work with agents, comes from **getting the foundations right**, especially things like: 

- **Telemetry:** it's hard to improve things we don't measure and agents must receive detailed information about users activities and everything we consider useful for them to know. 
- **User feedback:** if that's possible, we should seek a direct contact with our users, either on customer support chat, calls or offline meetings. Extremely useful context comes from analyzing bug reports and feature requests, but also transcripts from calls with people who never became our clients.  
- **Business context:** getting the right primitives and their proper shape requires a deep understanding of the business objectives, it's direction, challenges and even insights from a market. 
- **Product analytics:** telemetry isn't the only source of valuable information about the users. Product managers may have insights on user profiles, similar products or a broad perspective of the entire product. 
- **Agent environment:** everything we've discussed in **S01E05**, configured on remote machines or in the cloud. Without this, thinking about autonomy should be set aside until the complete environment and the essential details that give agents freedom are in place.
- **Labs:** it's worth to make a habit of **creating a simple labs** for playing with ideas and iterating on concepts. These may be html & css apps with we talk through agents. Doing this will give us quick understanding of the feature we want to build and share the concepts with others. It's similar to artifacts available in Claude for example, but lab is build in into the application we build.
- **Beta channel:** it is worth considering beta channels or nightly version of the app where updates are often and features may come and go. That's especially useful during scaling phase of the development process.

In other words, getting real leverage requires us to look around and see [what we can do with the systems](https://www.agentready.org/), resources, and tools we already have, so we can prepare them both for higher velocity and for agents that will use them on behalf of users. And this must include not only the engineering context but also a broad product perspective. 

Then, depending on our product and business, it's worth at least considering bold roadmap decisions, which in some cases may include building an entirely new version of the system. But more often, we'll want to design a plan that will gradually, and then suddenly, shift the product towards the agentic era and autonomous development - and that's where the real leverage will come.

## Takeaways

Developing the mindset of a product engineer helps us look at our roles much more broadly, beyond writing code, fixing bugs and implementing features. Even if the organization we're working in isn't ready for the shift yet, it's in our interest to start looking at our work from a broader perspective. This will let us see that **autonomous development isn't the end of our jobs. It is another chapter.**

Product engineering as a role isn't new, and there are many books on the topic worth reading. One of them is the already mentioned **[Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal)**, and others include **[Empowered](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/53481975)**, **[The Mom Test](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/52283963)** and **[Shape Up](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/50776459-shape-up)**. I've read all of them, and even though they were written before the "ChatGPT era", they still carry valuable lessons for us engineers.
